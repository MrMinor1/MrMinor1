The most expensive retirement decision self-employed owners make is choosing the account that was easiest to open.

The 2026 numbers: the employee deferral limit is $24,500, rising to $32,500 at age 50 and up, with a larger $11,250 catch-up for ages 60 through 63. The combined employee-and-employer cap is $72,000, and compensation counted toward it is capped at $360,000.

The structural difference matters more than any of those figures. A SEP-IRA has a single lever — an employer contribution calculated as a percentage of earnings. A Solo 401(k) has two: that same employer percentage, plus a personal salary deferral. There is no employee side to a SEP at all.

For a sole proprietor netting $100,000, that works out to roughly $18,600 into a SEP against roughly $43,100 into a Solo 401(k) — the same employer contribution, plus the $24,500 deferral sitting on top. Identical income, identical year, about $24,500 of additional shelter available purely from plan design.

The timing is where I see real money lost. A plan can now be established as late as the return's due date including extensions, which sounds like permission to defer the decision. But for a sole proprietor the deferral election generally has to be in place by December 31 — and the deferral is the larger half of the opportunity. Wait until you're filing and you can still open a plan; you just can't fund the part that mattered.

Which makes this an August question rather than a filing-season one.

If you're running a SEP today: was that a decision, or the account you could open fastest?
