The average tax refund last season was about $3,300. For a business owner, that number should bother you.

A refund isn't a bonus — it's the return of an interest-free loan you extended to the government, in a year when working capital costs 8-12% if you have to borrow it. $3,300 parked with the IRS is $3,300 not smoothing payroll, not buying inventory at a discount, not sitting in a 4%+ money market.

Yet "I love a big refund" remains one of the most common things we hear. It's worth asking why: a refund feels like winning, and owing feels like losing — even when the economics run exactly the other way. Behavioral framing beats arithmetic, again.

The same pattern shows up all over small-business tax decisions:

• Sunday-night bookkeeping "saves money" — until you price the owner's hours honestly.
• The S-Corp election gets decided by paperwork aversion, not by the break-even between payroll costs and self-employment tax saved.
• December equipment purchases get justified "for the write-off," as if a deduction made anything free. It discounts a purchase; it never pays for it.

None of these calls is hard math. What's rare is anyone actually running the math instead of the feeling.

So, honest question for owners and finance folks: do you aim for a refund, or for zero — and is that a decision you made, or a default you inherited?
