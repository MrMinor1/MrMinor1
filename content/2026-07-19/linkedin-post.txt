Tax planning has a depreciation curve, and almost nobody prices it.

The same advisory conversation — same advisor, same strategies, same fee — is worth dramatically different amounts depending on the date it happens:

• In July, a retirement plan change has 165 days of contributions ahead of it. An S-Corp election has two full quarters of payroll to restructure. An equipment decision can be timed, not rushed.

• In December, those same strategies get two frantic weeks, executed alongside everyone else's frantic two weeks.

• In April, they're worth zero. The year is locked; the conversation is archaeology.

Yet the industry's entire rhythm is built around April — the one month when advice can no longer change anything. Compliance work gets scheduled; planning work gets whatever's left, which is usually nothing.

The businesses that break this pattern do one simple thing: they put a mid-year review on the calendar as a standing commitment, the same way they close their books monthly. Not because July has magic in it — because option value decays, and July is when the options are still cheap.

165 days remain in the 2026 tax year. The question isn't whether you'll have a planning conversation — everyone eventually does, even if it's with themselves at the filing deadline. The question is what it will still be able to buy.
